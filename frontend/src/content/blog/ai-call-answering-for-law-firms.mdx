## Why a missed call costs a law firm more than most businesses

Most small businesses lose a sale when they miss a call. A law firm loses a case, and a case is not a one-time sale. A personal injury client might be worth a fee in the tens of thousands. A family law client might stay with the firm for a year. An estate planning client might bring in their parents and their siblings after that. One missed call can be the first domino in a chain of revenue that never happens, and you never even know about it, because the caller never leaves a message. They just move on.

The timing makes it worse. People do not call lawyers during business hours on a calm Tuesday. They call when something just happened: the accident was this morning, the spouse just left, the police just called, the landlord just served the notice. That call comes at 7pm, or on a Saturday, or during the exact hour you are in a hearing. The firms that answer those calls get those clients. The firms that do not answer are not even in the running.

I hear the same story from firm owners again and again. They check their voicemail on Monday morning and find three messages from Friday evening. Two of the callers already hired someone else. The third one is calling back to say never mind, they found someone. The owner did nothing wrong. They were doing their job, in court or with a client, which is exactly when the phone rings. The problem is not effort. The problem is that one person cannot be in two places, and at a small firm, the phone is always the thing that loses.

## What AI call answering actually does for a firm

Let me be specific about what the system does, because the words "AI receptionist" mean different things to different people, and most of the confusion comes from that.

An AI call answering system for a law firm does four things. It answers every call, any hour, in your firm's voice, with a greeting you wrote. It runs a short intake: what kind of case is this, how urgent is it, what is your name and number, and a short version of the facts. It screens against the rules you set, so a case you never take gets a polite referral instead of a consultation booking. And it books a consultation into your real calendar, or takes a detailed message and sends you a written summary within minutes.

That is the whole job. It is not a lawyer. It does not give legal advice, it does not promise outcomes, and it does not negotiate. It does the front-desk work that a good intake paralegal does on a first call: listen, ask the right questions, write it down, and get the person to the right next step. The part that surprises firm owners is the summary. When a call ends, you get the caller's name and number, the practice area, the short version of the facts, and whether it is urgent, in writing. You can read it in thirty seconds between meetings and decide what to do. Compare that with voicemail, where you get a garbled name, half a phone number, and no idea what the case is about, and you have to call back cold and start from zero.

## What a good intake call sounds like

The intake script is the heart of this, and it is also where most firms get it wrong the first time, because they try to make the AI do too much. A good intake call asks a small number of questions and asks them in the right order. Here is the shape that works.

First, the greeting and the practice area. "Thanks for calling, this is the office of Smith and Associates. What kind of legal help are you looking for today?" That one question sorts the call into a bucket: personal injury, family law, criminal defense, estate planning, immigration, or something the firm does not handle. If it is something the firm does not handle, the call ends politely with a referral or a simple no, and nobody's time is wasted.

Second, urgency. "Is anyone hurt, or is there a deadline today or tomorrow?" This is the question that decides whether the caller needs a callback in ten minutes or a consultation next week. An arrest, an accident with injuries, a court date tomorrow: those go to the top of the pile. A will update or a question about a contract: those can wait for a scheduled call.

Third, the facts, briefly. "Can you tell me, in a sentence or two, what happened?" The AI is not taking a deposition. It is collecting enough for you to recognize the case when you read the summary. Two or three sentences is plenty, and the AI should stop the caller politely if they start telling their whole life story. That is what the consultation is for.

Fourth, the contact details, spelled back. Name, phone number, email if they want to give one. Spelling the name back matters more than it sounds, because a misspelled name on an intake sheet is a small disaster that wastes everyone's time later.

Fifth, the next step. If it is a case you take, book the consultation into your calendar and tell the caller exactly when it is, and what to bring. If it needs a human decision first, take a detailed message and promise a callback by a specific time, and then make sure that callback happens. A promise with a time attached is worth ten times a vague "we will get back to you."

That is the whole script. Five questions, a few minutes, and the caller hangs up feeling like they talked to a real office that has its act together. That feeling is the product. A caller who feels heard books a consultation. A caller who hits voicemail calls your competitor.

The in-house receptionist is still the gold standard during business hours, and I will not pretend otherwise. A good receptionist knows your clients by voice, knows which callers get through immediately, and knows when to interrupt you. No software does that. But a receptionist works forty hours a week, takes lunch, takes vacation, and gets sick. The other 128 hours of the week, your phone is on its own, and that is when the new-client calls come.

The answering service is the option most firms try first, and it is the one that disappoints most. A human at a call center answers politely and takes a message, which sounds fine until you realize the message says "John called about a case, please call back" and nothing else. No practice area, no urgency, no facts. You call back cold, and John has already talked to two other firms. The service did its job, which is answering the phone. It did not do your job, which is intake.

Voicemail is free, and it costs the most. Every study I have seen, and every firm owner I have talked to, says the same thing: most new callers who reach voicemail do not leave a message. They hang up and call the next firm on the list. Voicemail is not a phone strategy. It is the absence of one.

## Where it helps most: practice area by practice area

Not every practice area gets the same value from this, and it is worth being honest about that. The firms that get the most out of AI call answering are the ones where new clients call in distress, outside business hours, and the first firm to answer usually gets the client.

Personal injury is the clearest case. The call comes the day of the accident, often in the evening, often from a hospital or a tow truck. The caller is shaken, they want to know if they have a case, and they are calling three firms. The firm that answers, takes the facts, and books a consultation for the next morning wins. The firm that lets it go to voicemail never hears from them again.

Family law is close behind. The call comes after a fight, after a spouse leaves, after a custody scare. It is emotional, and it is often late at night. A calm voice that listens, asks the right questions, and books a consultation for the next day is exactly what that caller needs. A voicemail is exactly what they do not need.

Criminal defense is similar, with one difference: urgency is everything. An arrest does not wait for Monday. The intake script needs a rule for it, something like "if the caller says someone was just arrested, transfer to the on-call attorney or take an urgent message and flag it." That rule is a configuration choice, not a technology problem, and it is the kind of thing you set once and never think about again.

Immigration, estate planning, and real estate work a little differently. Those callers are less likely to be in crisis and more likely to be shopping around, comparing firms, asking about fees and timelines. The AI handles those calls well too, because it can answer the standard questions, quote the consultation fee if you want it quoted, and book the consultation. It is the same intake flow, just with a calmer caller on the other end.

The practice area that gets the least value is the one where almost all new business comes from referrals and almost nobody cold-calls. If every client walks in through a referral, the phone matters less. Even then, the referral still calls to book, and a missed booking call is still a missed client. But I will not oversell it: the value scales with how many new clients find you by phone.

## The lines it must not cross

This is the section firm owners ask me about most, and it deserves a straight answer. There are things an AI answering system must never do for a law firm, and any vendor who tells you otherwise is selling you a problem.

It must never give legal advice. Not "you probably have a case," not "the statute of limitations is two years," not "you should file for custody." The intake script answers questions about the firm, the consultation, the fees, and the process. It does not answer questions about the law. If a caller asks a legal question, the correct answer is "that is something the attorney will go over with you in your consultation," and then book the consultation. That line should be written into the script, in plain language, and tested before the system goes live.

It must never promise an outcome. "We will take your case" is a promise the AI cannot make. "We will review your case and call you back" is a promise it can keep. The difference is small in wording and huge in liability. Your intake script should never contain the words "we can help you with that" as a commitment. It should say "let me get the details so the attorney can review it."

It must never take a retainer or payment details over the phone on its own. Payment is a human conversation, with a signed agreement. The AI can explain the consultation fee and the payment options, and it can book the consultation where the payment conversation happens. That is far enough.

It must never pretend to be a lawyer, and it must never pretend to be a specific person at the firm. Callers should be told, in the greeting, that they are speaking with the firm's AI assistant. This is not just honesty, it is good business. Callers are fine talking to an AI when it is helpful and upfront. They are not fine finding out later that they were misled, and in a law firm, trust is the entire product.

## Confidentiality: what to ask any vendor

A law firm handles privileged information, and the phone system is where that information first arrives. Before you put any system on your phones, ask the vendor these questions, and do not accept vague answers.

First, are call recordings and transcripts used to train the vendor's models? The answer you want is no. Your clients' facts are not training data, and any vendor that trains on your calls is a vendor you should not use.

Second, where is the data stored, and who can see it? You want data stored with encryption, access limited to your firm, and a clear answer on retention: how long recordings are kept, and how you delete them. If the vendor cannot tell you the retention period in one sentence, that is your answer.

Third, what happens when a caller says something privileged? The practical answer is that the intake summary is treated like any other client communication: it goes to the attorney, it stays inside the firm's systems, and it is not shared with anyone else. The vendor's job is to make that easy, with summaries delivered to your email or your case management system, not sitting in a dashboard you never check.

Fourth, does it work with your case management system? The intake summary is only useful if it lands where you actually work. A summary that sits in a separate app is a summary you will stop reading. Ask whether it can send the summary to your email, or better, create a matter or a lead in the system you already use.

These are not exotic questions. Any serious vendor answers them in writing, quickly. If a vendor hedges on any of them, walk away. There are plenty of vendors who answer them cleanly.

## After hours is where the money is

I keep coming back to this because it is the part firm owners underestimate most. They think about the phone as a daytime problem, because they are at the office during the day and they see the phone ringing. The real problem is the phone at night, when nobody is there to see it ring.

Think about your own life. When did you last call a service business during business hours because you had free time? You call when the problem happens, and problems do not keep office hours. A car accident happens at 6pm. A spouse leaves at 9pm. A landlord serves a notice on a Saturday. The firms that answer those calls are the firms that sign those clients.

The math is simple and it is not close. If your firm gets ten new-client calls a week and half of them come outside business hours, that is five calls a week that your daytime receptionist never sees. If you answer those five and convert two of them into clients, that is eight new clients a month you were not getting before. At even a modest fee per matter, that pays for the system many times over, and it keeps paying every month, because the phone keeps ringing at night.

This is also why the "we will call them back Monday" plan fails. By Monday, the caller has talked to two other firms, and one of them answered on Friday night. Monday is too late. The call needs to be answered when it comes in, by something that is always there.

## Setting it up for your firm

Setup takes about an hour of your time, and you are live within 48 hours. The work is mostly you talking about your own firm, which you already know. If you want the full timeline, I wrote about [how the setup works](/how-it-works) in detail. Here is the law-firm version, in short.

First, write down your intake script. Not a legal document, just the questions: what practice areas you take, what counts as urgent, what you charge for a consultation, and what a caller should bring. If you have an intake sheet your staff already uses, that is the script. Hand it over and the system uses it.

Second, set the transfer rules. Who gets called when a caller says someone was just arrested? Who gets the message when a caller says there is a court date tomorrow? Most firms pick one attorney as the urgent contact and one number as the fallback. Two decisions, and you are done.

Third, connect your calendar. The AI books consultations into your real open slots, so a caller leaves the call with a date and time, not a promise that someone will call them back to schedule. That one detail is the difference between a booked consultation and a caller who never comes back.

Fourth, decide the greeting. Use your firm's name, say they are speaking with the firm's AI assistant, and keep it warm. "Thank you for calling Smith and Associates. I am the firm's assistant, and I can help you book a consultation or take a message for the team." That is all it needs to be. Callers are fine with it when it is upfront and helpful.

Fifth, make test calls. Call it yourself and pretend to be a new client. Try to confuse it. Ask a legal question and check that it declines politely and books the consultation. Try the urgent scenario and check that the transfer rule fires. Fix the two or three things that sound off, and you are live. The first week is a tuning window: you read the summaries, fix the wording, and by week two it sounds like your firm.

A law firm's phone is its front door, and for a lot of small firms it is the only front door that matters. The calls come when you are in court, in a meeting, or at home for the evening, and the caller is choosing between you and the next firm on the list. The firm that answers gets the client. The firm that does not answer never hears from them again.

AI call answering does not replace your judgment, your staff, or your lawyers. It answers every call, runs a short intake, books the consultation, and sends you a written summary, any hour of the day. One signed case a month covers it for the year. The rest is clients you were already losing and did not know about.

If your phone is going to voicemail after hours, or while you are in court, that is the problem to fix first. Everything else about the firm's marketing can wait. The callers are already calling. The question is only whether someone answers.
